I have spent my career building brands across both B2B and B2C. That includes eight years partnering with Nike, and the kind of decision-making that drives growth: part instinct, from years in the room with the best brands and the people who run them, and part method, from taking teams and organisations from idea to execution. I try to lead in a human way, present for the big moments and the small ones, because that is what earns a team's trust and brings them with you.

I trained as a board director through Styrelseakademien, the Swedish academy for board governance, completing its Effective Company Directorship program in board leadership and governance. Alongside running businesses with full P&L, I have served as a chairman. I help boards and leadership teams set direction, govern well, and hold ambition and delivery in the same view. If you want a senior pair of hands in the boardroom, that is where I can help.

### Brands now have to be legible to machines
*Jun 16 2026*
*Description: Reputation used to be built for people. Increasingly it is read by agents. That changes what a brand has to be.*

For most of its history, a brand was something you built for people. The logo, the line, the way the phone got answered — all of it aimed at a human forming an impression.

That audience is no longer the only one that matters. More of the decisions about your brand are now made by machines acting on someone's behalf: which option an agent surfaces, which it skips, what it claims you stand for when asked. If a model can't read your brand cleanly, it will guess. And it will guess in the direction of whoever made themselves easiest to read.

This is not a creative problem. It is an operational one. The brands that win the next decade will be the ones that are legible — to people and to the systems increasingly standing between you and people. Consistent, machine-readable, unambiguous about what they are and, just as importantly, what they are not.

The work is less glamorous than a campaign and more consequential. It is infrastructure. And infrastructure is exactly the kind of thing that looks optional right up until the moment it isn't.

### The myth of the star creative
*Jun 14 2026*
*Description: The best work comes out of teams that share the credit. Star systems quietly tax the thing that made the work good.*

There is a persistent story in this industry that great work comes from great individuals. It is a flattering story, and it is mostly wrong.

The work I am proudest of came out of teams where the credit was collective and nobody was counting. That is not sentiment. It is mechanism. When credit is shared, people take risks they would not take if their name were on the line alone. They hand off, they build on each other, they let the better idea win regardless of whose it was. Star systems quietly tax all of that. They make people protective of ideas that should be common property.

Commercial change is supposed to make an organisation stronger. Too often it does the opposite — it strips out the culture that made the work worth doing in the first place, in the name of efficiency or visibility. The harder discipline is to change what needs changing while protecting that culture deliberately.

You can build a brand on a star. You cannot build an organisation on one.

### Scaling without losing the edge
*Jun 12 2026*
*Description: Commercial discipline and creative edge are usually framed as opposites. The whole job is refusing that trade.*

The standard story about growth is a trade-off. You can have creative edge or you can have commercial discipline, and as an organisation scales it slowly trades the first for the second. Process accretes, risk gets managed out, and one day you look up and the work is competent and forgettable.

I don't accept the trade. Across six markets at B-Reel, the growth that held up didn't come from choosing discipline over edge. It came from introducing enough discipline that the edge had room to operate. Financial clarity isn't the enemy of good work. Chaos is. Teams that can't see the commercial picture make worse creative decisions, not braver ones.

The actual skill is integration: getting strategy, creative, product and operations to behave as one organisation rather than four departments defending turf. That's unglamorous, and it's most of the job. Do it well and the financial performance and the work improve together. Do it badly and you get a business that's either broke or boring.

Scale is not where edge goes to die. It's where weak organisations reveal they never had the discipline to keep it alive.
